## 1. Strategic Summary

This episode tests whether the office can explain a tense contrast without creating a rule avalanche.

The topic:

```text
Past Simple vs Present Perfect
```

The angle is not:

```text
learn all uses of Present Perfect.
```

The angle is:

```text
Stop asking only "when did it happen?" Ask whether the past is still connected to now.
```

Core mental model:

```text
Past Simple = finished event in a finished time box.
Present Perfect = past event, experience, or result connected to now.
```

One strong thing:

```text
Past Simple closes the folder. Present Perfect keeps it open on the desk.
```

## 6. Full Teleprompter Script

### Hook

English has a tense that makes Russian-speaking learners quietly question reality.

Present Perfect.

You see:

```text
I lost my keys.
I've lost my keys.
```

And your brain says:

"Both happened before now. Why are there two doors?"

Good question.

Here is the simple version.

Past Simple says:

```text
This happened in a finished time box.
```

Present Perfect says:

```text
This happened before now, but it still touches now.
```

Past Simple closes the folder.

Present Perfect keeps the folder open on the desk.

Today I will show you how to choose without turning your brain into a tense committee.

### Promise Receipt

Look at this:

```text
I lost my keys yesterday.
```

That is Past Simple.

Why?

Because:

```text
yesterday
```

is a finished time box.

Now look:

```text
I've lost my keys.
```

Why Present Perfect?

Because the result matters now.

I do not have the keys now.

I am standing outside now.

My dignity is waiting in the hallway now.

Same kind of event.

Different camera.

Past Simple asks:

```text
When did it happen?
```

Present Perfect asks:

```text
Why does it matter now?
```

That is the receipt.

You clicked for the difference.

Here it is:

```text
finished time box
vs
connection to now
```

### The Russian Trap

Russian often lets you talk about the past without forcing this exact choice.

So your Russian brain sees:

```text
потерял
был
видел
закончил
```

and thinks:

"Past. Done. Give me Past Simple."

English says:

Not so fast.

English asks:

```text
Is the time finished?
Or is the result alive now?
```

The question is not only:

```text
Did it happen before now?
```

The better question is:

```text
Is the past event connected to now?
```

Professor Lingman verdict:

```text
Present Perfect is not being difficult. It is just asking for context like a dramatic detective.
```

### The Decision Model

Use this two-question test.

Question one:

```text
Do I mention a finished time?
```

Words like:

```text
yesterday
last week
in 2020
two days ago
when I was a child
```

usually pull you to Past Simple.

```text
I saw this movie yesterday.
She finished the report last week.
We met in 2020.
```

Question two:

```text
Is the result, experience, or relevance connected to now?
```

Then Present Perfect often appears.

```text
I've seen this movie.
She's finished the report.
We've met before.
```

Notice the difference.

```text
I saw this movie yesterday.
```

The time is specific.

Story closed.

```text
I've seen this movie.
```

The experience matters now.

Maybe we are choosing what to watch.

Maybe I am saying:

"No need. This movie has already damaged me once."

### Contrast Pair 1 - Lost Keys

Pair one:

```text
I lost my keys yesterday.
I've lost my keys.
```

Past Simple:

```text
I lost my keys yesterday.
```

You are telling a past story.

Maybe you found them later.

Maybe the drama is over.

Present Perfect:

```text
I've lost my keys.
```

The result matters now.

I cannot open the door.

I need help.

The problem is alive.

### Contrast Pair 2 - Paris

Pair two:

```text
I went to Paris in 2022.
I've been to Paris.
```

Past Simple:

```text
I went to Paris in 2022.
```

Specific finished time.

Present Perfect:

```text
I've been to Paris.
```

Life experience.

The exact time is not the point.

The point is:

I have that experience now.

So if someone asks:

```text
Have you ever been to Paris?
```

You answer:

```text
Yes, I've been to Paris.
```

Not because Paris is floating in the present.

Because the experience is part of your present life.

### Contrast Pair 3 - Finished Work

Pair three:

```text
I finished the report yesterday.
I've finished the report.
```

Past Simple:

```text
I finished the report yesterday.
```

Specific finished time.

Present Perfect:

```text
I've finished the report.
```

Result now:

The report is ready.

You can read it.

I can stop pretending to enjoy spreadsheets.

### Micro-Test

Quick test.

Choose the better sentence.

```text
1. I saw him yesterday.
   I've seen him yesterday.

2. I have finished the task.
   I finished the task last night.

3. Did you ever try sushi?
   Have you ever tried sushi?

4. She went home two hours ago.
   She has gone home two hours ago.

5. I've lost my phone.
   I lost my phone in 2019.
```

Answers:

```text
1. I saw him yesterday.
2. "I finished the task last night" if you mention last night. "I've finished the task" if the result matters now.
3. Have you ever tried sushi?
4. She went home two hours ago.
5. Both can be right in different contexts.
```

Do you see the pattern?

Specific finished time:

```text
Past Simple.
```

Experience or result connected to now:

```text
Present Perfect.
```

### Common Traps

Trap one:

```text
I've seen him yesterday.
```

Usually wrong.

Why?

Because:

```text
yesterday
```

is a finished time box.

Say:

```text
I saw him yesterday.
```

Trap two:

```text
Did you ever try sushi?
```

In many standard contexts, the natural question is:

```text
Have you ever tried sushi?
```

Why?

Life experience up to now.

Trap three:

```text
I have finished it last night.
```

No.

```text
last night
```

pulls you into Past Simple.

Say:

```text
I finished it last night.
```

Or remove the finished time:

```text
I've finished it.
```

Now the result matters.

### Edge Case Without Panic

Small warning.

American English sometimes uses Past Simple in places where British English may prefer Present Perfect.

For example:

```text
Did you eat yet?
Have you eaten yet?
```

Both can be heard.

Do not panic.

This video is not trying to turn you into an international tense lawyer.

Use the main model first:

```text
finished time box
or
connection to now
```

Then learn common chunks and local usage.

### One-Sentence Payoff

If you remember nothing else:

```text
Past Simple closes the time box. Present Perfect keeps the past connected to now.
```

So:

```text
I lost my keys yesterday.
```

But:

```text
I've lost my keys.
```

One is a past story.

One is a present problem.

## 9. Description

Past Simple and Present Perfect are not just two ways to talk about the past.

If you speak Russian, your brain may want to use Past Simple for everything that happened before now. But English often asks a different question: is the past event finished inside a time box, or is it still connected to now?

In this lesson, Professor Lingman explains:

```text
why "I saw him yesterday" is natural;
why "I've seen him yesterday" usually sounds wrong;
why "I've lost my keys" means the result matters now;
how life experience uses Present Perfect;
how finished time words push you toward Past Simple;
how to use a simple decision test when speaking.
```

After watching, practice contrast pairs in Phraseman so the tense choice becomes a reflex, not a slow grammar debate.
